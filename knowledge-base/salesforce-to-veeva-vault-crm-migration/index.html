Veeva CRM Is Leaving Salesforce. What You Do Next Decides Your AI Layer.

Veeva CRM is leaving Salesforce before 2030. Every company on it has to pick a destination, and the AI plan rides on the choice. What moves and where to start.

Contents

    For most of its life, Veeva CRM has been a Salesforce application. The objects, the Apex, the reports, the mobile app, the integrations, the admin skills: all of it sat on the Salesforce platform. Veeva is now moving CRM onto its own Vault platform, and it has said that support for the Salesforce-based version runs through 2030.

    That sounds like a long runway. It is not. Every life sciences company running Veeva CRM has to make the same move in the same window, using the same limited pool of people who have done it before. The companies that size the work now get to choose their timeline. The ones that wait will take whatever timeline the market has left.

    This is written for the commercial and IT leaders at small and mid-size pharma and biotech who own that decision. Large pharma has program offices for this. You have a launch calendar, a renewal date, and a board that wants to hear an AI plan. The migration has to fit around all three.

    And it is more than a platform move. It is the moment your AI layer gets decided, whether or not anyone in the room says so.

    Two vendors, one data foundation

    While you are being asked to migrate, both vendors are racing to own AI for the field. Veeva is building its AI into Vault CRM. Salesforce is building Agentforce and Data Cloud into Life Sciences Cloud and offering it as the alternative destination. The systems integrators are picking sides and certifying accordingly, because that is how partner programs work.

    You do not get a clean side. For most incumbents the field lands on Vault CRM; for companies launching fresh, or already deep in Health Cloud, Life Sciences Cloud is a real alternative. Patient services, if it grew up on Health Cloud, has good reasons to stay on Salesforce either way; it is the part of the org Veeva does not move. The warehouse, the MDM, and the analytics layer belong to neither. The AI a rep actually wants, the kind that knows the account, the last three calls, the open medical inquiry, and the enrollment status of the patient they are about to discuss, needs data from all of it.

    Every vendor deck says the same thing in its own vocabulary: agents only work on a unified data foundation. Delete the product name and that sentence is true on both sides of the fence. The practical consequence is that whoever does your migration is laying the foundation every future agent runs on, whichever vendor’s agent it turns out to be. Treat the migration as that, and the AI plan the board wants stops being a separate slide.

    There is a regulatory reason to get this right as well. The EU AI Act and the FDA’s guidance on AI credibility both point the same direction: AI touching regulated work needs auditability, human oversight, and evidence. That argues for a specific architecture. Put deterministic, auditable logic in the integration layer and on the client, use models where they add real value, and generate the validation evidence as you build rather than assembling it at the end. We wrote about the client-side half of that pattern in Applied Innovation: AI Hybrid CRM Experience.

    It is not a CRM migration. It is an org migration.

    The first thing to understand is that your Salesforce org was never only Veeva CRM.

    Over the years, that org accumulated things. Custom objects for key account management. Apex triggers someone wrote in 2019 to fix a territory problem. Flows that route medical inquiries. A patient services team that quietly built their program on the same instance because it was there. Integrations to the data warehouse, the MDM, the expense system, the speaker bureau vendor. Reports that the commercial VP reads every Monday.

    Veeva will help you move Veeva CRM. Nobody is going to move the rest of the org for you, and the rest of the org is where the cost and the risk live.

    Before anyone talks about timelines, inventory the org and put every component into one of four buckets:

    1. Moves to Vault CRM as configuration. Standard Veeva objects, fields, and page layouts that map to their Vault equivalents. This is the part the vendor tooling covers.
    2. Gets rebuilt on the Vault platform. Custom logic that has to survive. Vault has no Apex; customization is configuration plus the Vault Java SDK. Anything you rebuild, you rebuild deliberately, and most of it should be smaller than what it replaces.
    3. Stays on Salesforce, on an org you now pay for separately. The patient services program, the aesthetics sales team on Sales Cloud, whatever else took root. This bucket has a license cost that did not exist before, and it needs an owner before the migration starts, not after.
    4. Retires. Every org has a third of its customizations doing nothing. The migration is the one time you can prove it and remove them without a fight.

    The size of buckets two and three is the size of your migration. The vendor estimate only covers bucket one.

    What actually moves

    Once the org is inventoried, the data and content picture is more predictable.

    Accounts and HCP data. If you are already on Veeva Network or OpenData, the master is in good shape and the move is mostly about local custom fields. If your HCP master is the Salesforce account object with years of rep-entered edits, clean it before you move it. Migrating bad data onto a new platform makes it permanent.

    Call history, activity, and samples. Decide how much history comes across. Sample disbursement records carry regulatory retention requirements and have to move with their audit trail intact. Five years of call notes for reps who left in 2021 probably do not.

    Territories and alignments. If Veeva Align is already the source of truth, this is straightforward. If territory rules live in Salesforce assignment logic and Apex, they go in bucket two.

    Approved Email and CLM content. Already in Vault PromoMats. This is the easiest part of the move and the one people overestimate.

    Events, medical inquiries, key accounts. Each of these is either a Veeva module with a Vault destination, a custom build that needs a decision, or a gap you fill with something else. Do not assume.

    Reports and dashboards. Salesforce reporting does not come with you. Every report the business depends on has to be rebuilt in Vault reporting, in your warehouse, or in whatever analytics layer you choose. This is the item most often left off the plan and most often noticed first by the executive team.

    Integrations. Every system that talks to the org through the Salesforce API has to be repointed to the Vault API, retested, and revalidated where validation applies. Count them. The number is always higher than the first answer.

    What drives the number

    Migration calculators exist for this move. Most take a module count and a country count, multiply, and produce a budget band. That is not sizing. That is arithmetic.

    The variables that actually move the cost, in the order we have seen them matter:

    • Custom code volume. Lines of Apex and number of custom objects are the single best predictor of effort, because each one is a decision as well as a rebuild.
    • Integration count and ownership. Ten integrations owned by three vendors and one departed contractor cost more than twenty owned by your own team.
    • Data quality. Time spent cleaning before migration is cheap. Time spent explaining to reps why their accounts look wrong after migration is not.
    • Countries and languages. Each market adds validation, training, and a cutover weekend.
    • The Salesforce remainder. Bucket three, above. Some companies discover a second migration inside the first.
    • Parallel run. How long you pay for both platforms is a decision, and the default is “longer than planned.”
    • Validation posture. Where 21 CFR Part 11 or equivalent applies, evidence has to be generated as you build, not assembled at the end.

    Field rep retraining is real but it is not where the money goes. The money goes to buckets two and three.

    The order of operations

    Do the work in this sequence. The sequence matters more than the tooling.

    Inventory and sizing. Four buckets, every component, every integration, every report. This takes weeks, not months, if someone who has done it is driving.

    Data foundation. Clean the HCP master. Decide history retention. Fix the alignment source of truth. Nothing built on top of bad data improves the data.

    One market, end to end. Pick a pilot market that is small enough to fail safely and representative enough to prove the pattern. Take it all the way through cutover and hypercare. Every assumption in the plan gets tested here.

    Commercial core. Roll the proven pattern across the primary markets in waves sized by validation and training capacity.

    Expansion. Remaining markets, secondary modules, the Salesforce remainder, retirement of the old org.

    AI, designed first and built last. Decide during inventory which conversations you want an agent to have, what data each one needs, and where the human stays in the loop. That decides what the data foundation has to contain and which integrations are on the critical path. Then build the agents after the foundation exists, on whichever platform’s tooling fits, so they work on the first day rather than on a roadmap.

    Where it goes wrong

    We keep seeing the same failure modes.

    The migration is treated as a vendor project. Veeva moves Veeva; nobody owns the rest. Six months in, the patient services team finds out their program is losing its platform.

    The plan starts with the largest market because it matters most. The largest market is where an untested pattern does the most damage.

    Custom code is lifted and shifted. Every Apex trigger becomes a Vault SDK job, and the new platform inherits ten years of workarounds on day one.

    Reports are discovered at UAT.

    Validation is scheduled as a phase at the end, which means it is the phase that slips.

    Nobody sets a date to turn off the Salesforce licenses, so the parallel run becomes a permanent line item.

    Each of these is avoidable, and all of them are avoided the same way: by sizing honestly before anyone commits to a date.

    The first ninety days

    You do not need a multi-year program plan to start. You need three things by the end of the first quarter.

    1. The inventory, with every component in a bucket and every integration named with an owner.
    2. A sizing, in bands, with the drivers above scored rather than guessed.
    3. A pilot market and a cutover date for it, so the program has a real first milestone instead of a slide.

    With those three things you can negotiate the Salesforce renewal from a position of knowledge, give the board a roadmap that includes the AI plan, and set a timeline you chose rather than one you were left with.

    Where we sit

    We are not a Veeva practice or a Salesforce practice. We do not sell either license, and we are not certifying our way into either partner program. We are the engineering team in the middle: the migration work, the integration layer that spans Vault and whatever stays on Salesforce, and the conversational, real-time experience on top that reps and patient services teams actually use. That layer has to work the same whether the record lands in Vault or in Salesforce, and building it that way is the whole point.

    The intake pattern is the clearest example. A conversation or a document comes in, structured data comes out, and it lands in the system of record in one transaction with the audit trail intact. It is how a patient gets enrolled, how a medical inquiry gets logged, and how an HCP gets onboarded. It is also how the accelerator below works. Same pattern, smaller stakes.
